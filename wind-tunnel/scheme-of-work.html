WS1 · Overview01 / 09

Worksheet 1 — Pitot-static Tube

The pitot-static tube measures the speed of a fluid by comparing two pressures: the pitot pressure (the tube pointing into the flow) and the static pressure (around the tube). The faster the air, the larger the difference between them.

Learning objectives

  • Explain how a pitot-static tube measures fluid speed
  • Take air-speed measurements using a U-tube manometer and a digital pressure sensor
  • Apply Bernoulli's equation to convert pressure difference into velocity
  • Calculate the Reynolds number for each operating point
Where this fitsWeek 3 of the BTEC scheme — the lab that anchors the theory of static, dynamic and total pressure introduced in the Bernoulli lecture.
WS1 · Theory02 / 09

How a pitot-static tube works

A pitot-static tube has two openings:

  • The pitot port points directly into the air flow. Moving air is brought to rest at this port — its kinetic energy converts into a small extra pressure called dynamic pressure.
  • The static port(s) sit perpendicular to the flow. They sense only the static pressure of the surrounding air.

Subtracting one from the other gives the dynamic pressure on its own, and Bernoulli's equation relates that directly to velocity.

Ptotal = Pstatic + ½ ρ V²Rearranging for V from Δp = Ptotal − Pstatic:
V = √( 2 Δp / ρ )Δp in Pa · ρ = 1.20 kg·m⁻³

What you'll need

  • Matrix Wind Tunnel 125 with touch-screen controller

  • U-tube manometer (filled with water to about 50 mm)
  • Built-in digital pressure meter on the controller
  • Stopwatch (optional, for steady-state checks)
  • Lab notebook / Student Handout sheet
SafetyNever run the fan with the test section unsecured. Keep loose hair, sleeves and cables clear of the inlet grille. Switch off before changing connections.

WS1 · Procedure · Part 104 / 09

Reading air speed with a U-tube manometer

  1. Place the pitot-static holder into the test section. Secure with both thumb-nuts. The tube must point directly into the flow.
  2. Add water to the manometer via a side port so the water sits near the 50 mm mark.
  3. Connect the holder to the manometer ports.
  4. With the fan off (0%), record both column heights.
  5. Switch on the fan, set to 10% on the touch-screen.
  6. Read and record both column heights.
  7. Increase the fan speed in 10% steps up to 100%, recording the column heights at each step.
WS1 · Analysis · Part 105 / 09

From column height to air speed

The pressure difference across the manometer is the weight of the water column. For each fan speed:

Δp = ρw · g · hρw = 1000 kg·m⁻³ · g = 9.81 N·kg⁻¹ · h = height difference in metres

Substituting into the velocity expression gives:

V² = 2 ρw · g · h / ρairwith ρair = 1.20 kg·m⁻³

For each fan-speed value, calculate h in metres and then V in m·s⁻¹. Plot a graph of air speed against height difference.

WS1 · Procedure · Part 206 / 09

Reading air speed with the digital sensor

  1. Detach the tubes from the manometer. Connect them to the two pressure ports on the wind tunnel control unit.
  2. With the fan off, zero the sensors from the touch-screen.
  3. Set the fan to 10%. Read air speed and pressure difference from the digital meter.
  4. Step the fan up to 100% in 10% increments, recording both readings each time.
Why both methods?Comparing the manometer (manual, mechanical) with the digital sensor (electronic) trains learners in the trade-off between resolution, response time and human-reading error — a key BTEC technical skill.
WS1 · Analysis · Part 207 / 09

Plotting and comparing

  • Plot a second graph: air speed vs pressure difference, from the digital meter.
  • Compare the two graphs. They should agree within experimental error — comment on which method is more precise and which is faster.

Challenge — Reynolds number

Re = ρ V L / μρ = 1.20 kg·m⁻³ · μ = 1.81 × 10⁻⁵ Pa·s · L = characteristic length

Comment on the regime (laminar, transitional, turbulent) the tunnel achieves at different fan settings.

WS2 · Overview01 / 09

Worksheet 2 — Flow Around a Cylinder

Designers reduce drag on ships, cars and aircraft by shaping the body to control the flow around it. Two factors matter most: the profile the body presents to the flow and the speed of the flow (captured by Reynolds number).

To quantify how pressure varies around any shape, engineers use the dimensionless pressure coefficient Cp. Here you measure Cp around a cylinder and produce a pressure-distribution diagram.

Learning objectives

  • Define and calculate the pressure coefficient Cp
  • Record pressure across angle of attack 0° → 180° in 5° steps
  • Produce a Cp-vs-angle graph and a pressure-distribution diagram
  • Compare experimental results to the theoretical inviscid-flow curve
WS2 · Theory02 / 09

Pressure coefficient Cp

Cp compares the local pressure on a surface to the free-stream conditions, non-dimensionalised by the dynamic pressure.

Cp = (P − P∞) / (½ ρ V²)P = local pressure (Pa) · P∞ = free-stream pressure (from WS1) · ρ = 1.20 kg·m⁻³ · V = free-stream velocity

Reynolds number — why scaling matters

Two bodies of different sizes will produce the same flow pattern only if their Reynolds numbers match. For the cylinder:

Re = ρ V D / μD = cylinder diameter · μ = 1.81 × 10⁻⁵ Pa·s
WS2 · Equipment03 / 09

What you'll need

  • Matrix Wind Tunnel 125
  • Tapped cylinder attached to a circular protractor (5° resolution)
  • Two thumb-nuts to secure the holder
  • Digital pressure sensor (built into the controller)
  • WS1 results — the free-stream pressure P∞ for each fan speed
Setup tipLine up the 90° mark on the protractor with the vertical marker on the test window. This sets your zero-angle reference.

Open the User Manual at pp. 7–9 → for the holder fitting diagram and twisting mechanism.

WS2 · Procedure04 / 09

Sweeping the angle of attack

  1. Place the cylinder holder into the test section. Secure with thumb-nuts.
  2. Rotate to angle of attack α = 0°.
  3. Connect the pressure port to one port on the control unit. Leave the other open to ambient.
  4. Zero the pressure sensor with the fan off.
  5. Turn the fan on. Set to 20%.
  6. Read and record the air speed and pressure.
  7. Rotate to α = 5°. Repeat reading.
  8. Continue in 5° steps up to α = 180°, recording at every position.
WS2 · Analysis05 / 09

Calculating Cp and plotting

  • For each angle, calculate Cp. Both pressure values should be negative — record magnitudes carefully.
  • Plot a graph of Cp vs angle of attack α for the 20% fan speed.

Compare to theory

The theoretical curve for inviscid flow around a cylinder:

Cp(α) = 1 − 4 sin²(α)

Compare your measured curve to this. Where do they diverge? Usual reasons: viscous boundary layers, flow separation behind the cylinder, wake effects.

WS2 · Pressure Distribution06 / 09

Drawing the pressure-distribution diagram

Engineers present pressure data as arrows around the body:

  • Negative pressure — arrows point away from the body (suction)
  • Positive pressure — arrows point towards the body

Use your Cp values to draw arrows on the template. Mirror the symmetric data for the other side.

Why this mattersPressure-distribution diagrams are how aerodynamicists communicate where lift and drag come from. You'll meet them again in WS3 for the aerofoil.
WS2 · Challenge07 / 09

Reynolds-number sweep

Repeat the whole procedure at fan speeds of 50%, 80% and 100%. Calculate Reynolds number at each, and overlay the Cp curves.

  • Do the curves collapse onto each other?
  • If not, what does that tell you about Cp theory?
  • Look for changes in the separation point as Reynolds increases.
Open question for the reportAt what Reynolds number does the cylinder's wake first show clearly turbulent behaviour in this rig? Cross-check against published cylinder-drag-crisis data.

WS3 · Overview01 / 09

Worksheet 3 — Aerofoil Pressure Mapping

When fluid flows around an object, it can generate net forces that cause the object to rise or fall. The shape (profile) the body presents to the flow determines the size of these forces. An aerofoil exploits this — giving aircraft lift, wind turbines rotation and ship propellers thrust.

In this worksheet you'll measure the pressure at 23 points along a NACA 0015 aerofoil and plot how the pressure varies along its surface.

Learning objectives

  • Measure static pressure at 23 tappings along the chord of an aerofoil
  • Calculate Cp at each tapping and plot Cp vs fractional chord
  • Compare upper- and lower-surface pressure distributions
  • See how angle of attack changes the pressure profile and the lift it produces
WS3 · Theory02 / 09

Why pressure mapping reveals lift

The flow over the upper surface is accelerated; Bernoulli tells us that means lower pressure there. Beneath the aerofoil, flow is slower and pressure higher. The net upward force is lift.

Cp = (P − P∞) / (½ ρ V²)P = pressure at tapping · P∞ = free-stream pressure (from WS1)

NACA 0015 — symmetric profile

The first two digits "00" mean no camber (symmetric); "15" means thickness-to-chord = 15%. At α = 0° it should produce essentially no lift. As α increases, the top-surface pressure drops sharply and lift develops.

What you'll need

WS3 · Procedure05 / 09

Step by step

  1. Place the aerofoil holder into the test section and secure with thumb-nuts.
  2. Set the angle of attack to α = 0°.
  3. Zero the pressure sensors with the fan off.
  4. Switch on the fan and set to 25%.
  5. Connect tapping 1 to port 1. Leave port 2 open to ambient.
  6. Read and record the air speed and pressure for that point.
  7. Disconnect tapping 1 and repeat for tappings 2 through 23 in turn.
  8. Set α = 5° and repeat all 23 readings.
  9. Finally set α = 12° and repeat once more.
Why these angles?0° is the baseline, 5° is a typical cruise angle and 12° is close to (or past) the stall point for NACA 0015.
WS3 · Analysis06 / 09

From pressure to Cp-vs-x graph

  • For each tapping, calculate Cp.
  • Plot Cp vs fractional chord position x. On the same axes, plot top-surface and lower-surface curves.
  • Repeat for α = 5° and α = 12°.
What to look forThe area between upper and lower curves is proportional to CL. At α = 0° on a symmetric aerofoil the two curves should coincide. At α = 12° you should see a sharp suction peak near the leading edge that may have begun to collapse — the onset of stall.

  • Inward arrows (towards the body) for positive Cp

WS4 · Overview01 / 09

Worksheet 4 — Lift & Drag on an Aerofoil

WS3 inferred lift from a pressure map. This worksheet measures lift and drag directly with built-in load cells.

  • Sweep angle of attack from −25° to +45° in 5° steps
  • At three fan speeds: 20%, 50%, 80%
  • For three aerofoil profiles: NACA 0015, S1223, NACA 4415
  • Then compare their lift / drag characteristics on the same axes

Learning objectives

  • Use the two-force module to capture lift and drag forces
  • Plot lift- and drag-vs-α curves at multiple air speeds
  • Identify the stall angle from the lift curve
  • Compare three aerofoils and select an appropriate one for a given application
WS4 · Theory02 / 09

The lift equation

L = ½ ρ V² S CLS = aerofoil planform area · CL = lift coefficient (depends on shape, α, Reynolds)

Drag follows the same pattern. The lift / drag ratio CL/CD is a key efficiency metric — a glider wants it large, a Formula 1 wing wants the opposite.

Stall

As angle of attack rises, lift grows linearly — until the flow on the top surface separates. CL drops sharply and drag spikes. That angle is the stall angle.

WS4 · Equipment03 / 09

The two-force module

  • Two built-in load cells: one measures lift, the other drag
  • Stepper motor sets the angle of attack precisely
  • Three aerofoils with threaded inserts: NACA 0015 (symmetric), S1223 (high-lift Selig), NACA 4415 (cambered)
  • Cables linking the holder to the wind tunnel control unit

Open the User Manual at pp. 13–14 → for connection diagrams and the mounting procedure.

WS4 · Procedure04 / 09

Step by step — NACA 0015

  1. Thread the NACA 0015 onto the two-force holder. Mount in the test section.
  2. Connect both holder sockets to the control unit.
  3. Set α = 0°. With the fan off, zero both load cells.
  4. Switch the fan on and set to 20%.
  5. Sweep α from −25° to +45° in 5° steps. Record lift and drag at each angle.
  6. Repeat at 50% fan.
  7. Repeat at 80% fan.
  8. Switch off. Swap to the S1223. Repeat steps 3–7.
  9. Swap to the NACA 4415. Repeat once more.

  • On a second set of axes, plot drag force vs α for the same three speeds.

Questions

  • At what angle does lift on the NACA 0015 peak? What's the drag doing there?

WS4 · Comparing Aerofoils06 / 09

Three aerofoils, side by side

Collect all three aerofoils' results at the same fan speed (20%):

  • Plot a second graph of drag vs α with the same three.

WS4 · Application07 / 09

Picking the right aerofoil for the job

WS5 · Overview01 / 09

Worksheet 5 — Drag Coefficient

"Streamlined" is everyday speech for a shape that slips through a fluid with minimum resistance. In engineering we quantify that with the drag coefficient Cd.

You'll measure drag force on six reference shapes, calculate Cd for each, and compare with industry-standard values.

Learning objectives

  • Calculate the drag coefficient from measured force, velocity and reference area
  • Compare experimental and theoretical Cd for six standard shapes
  • Explain why a car's Cd is around 0.2–0.3, and why golf balls are dimpled
  • Understand that Cd can depend on Reynolds number, surface roughness and flow regime
WS5 · Theory02 / 09

The drag-coefficient formula

Cd = D / (½ ρ V² A)D = measured drag force (N) · ρ = 1.20 kg·m⁻³ · V = air speed · A = frontal area

For these test shapes, diameter d = 44 mm = 0.044 m. Frontal area A = πd²/4.

Why drag varies with shape

  • Form (pressure) drag — bigger when the body has a large wake. Reduced by streamlining the back.
  • Skin-friction drag — viscous drag along the surface. Smaller for shorter, smoother bodies.

WS6 · Overview01 / 08

Worksheet 6 — Streamlined?

Wind tunnels are used in industry to visualise the air flow around cars, aircraft and buildings — to improve their design. In this worksheet you'll inject a thin stream of smoke into the tunnel inlet and photograph the patterns produced.

Because the streams change rapidly, you'll capture both stills and slow-motion video.

Learning objectives

  • Operate the smoke generator safely and generate clean streamlines
  • Capture flow patterns with stills and slow-motion phone video
  • Identify regions of laminar and turbulent flow
  • Spot flow separation behind bluff bodies and how it shrinks with streamlining
WS6 · Theory02 / 08

What you'll see in the smoke

  • Laminar flow — smoke ribbons stay smooth and parallel.
  • Transition — the smooth ribbon develops small wobbles.
  • Turbulent flow — the ribbon breaks up into swirling eddies, mixing rapidly.
  • Separation — flow detaches from the surface behind a bluff body, leaving a low-pressure wake.
Why this mattersStreamlining a body shifts the separation point further back — shrinking the wake and reducing form drag. You measured exactly that with Cd in WS5; now you'll see it.
WS6 · Equipment03 / 08

What you'll need

  • Matrix Wind Tunnel 125
  • Smoke generator (oil-vapour mist), correctly filled and warm
  • Two-force module holder (so you can mount any test object firmly)
  • A selection of test objects — pick contrasting pairs
  • Phone with a slow-motion video setting

Open the User Manual at p. 17 → for smoke generator warm-up procedure and safety guidance.

SafetyThe smoke is a fine oil mist. Ventilate the room. Avoid prolonged inhalation.
WS6 · Procedure04 / 08

Step by step

  1. Mount your first test object firmly on the two-force module.
  2. Warm up the smoke generator according to the manual.
  3. Switch on the fan at a low speed first. (Too fast and the smoke is whipped away.)
  4. Hold the smoke outlet near the centre of the inlet grille.
  5. Adjust laterally to put the smoke onto the part of the body you want to inspect.
  6. Capture on your phone — slow-motion if available.
  7. Experiment with other fan speeds and other angles of attack.
  8. Switch objects and repeat.
WS6 · Capturing Flow05 / 08

Tips for clean imagery

  • Dark backdrop behind the test section.
  • Side lighting — a small LED torch shining perpendicular to your camera.
  • Slow-motion video at 120–240 fps stops the eddies clearly.
  • Steady camera — rest the phone or use a tripod.
  • One variable at a time — change object, speed or angle, not all at once.
WS6 · Analysis06 / 08

Annotating your captures

For each image, label:

  • Stagnation point — where the front streamline splits
  • Acceleration region — streamlines tight together
  • Boundary layer growth — gradual broadening next to the surface
  • Separation point — where the streamline first peels away
  • Wake — the disordered region downstream
Linking back to WS5For each bluff body where you measured Cd, you should now be able to see why a high Cd shape has a wide wake and a low Cd shape has a narrow one.
WS6 · Investigations07 / 08

Suggested studies

  • Streamlining a brick — flat plate vs teardrop; compare separation points.
  • Smooth vs rough sphere — visualise the WS5 drag-reduction directly.
  • Aerofoil at increasing α — watch the flow detach from the upper surface near stall.
  • Tandem bodies — place one body in another's wake.
  • Surface treatment — does tape on a sphere trip the boundary layer to turbulence earlier?

  • Connect the visual evidence to the Cd data from WS5 and the pressure data from WS3

WS7 · Design Rules03 / 08

Test article guidelines

  • Test area is 125 mm × 125 mm.
  • Frontal area must be less than 10% (< 1,560 mm² frontal).
  • Mount via a threaded insert compatible with the two-force module shaft.
  • For pressure measurements, include a hollow tapping connectable with tubing.
  • Wall thickness ≥ 1.5 mm; small fillet at the mount.
WorkflowSketch → CAD (Fusion 360 / OnShape) → check frontal area < 10% → 3D-print at 30% infill → tap-test for fit → low-speed sanity check.
WS7 · Pick a Procedure04 / 08

Match your question to a method

If your question is about pressure:

Use the method from WS2 (cylinder) or WS3 (aerofoil). Include surface tappings, connect them to the controller's pressure port one at a time, and compute Cp.

If your question is about forces:

Use the method from WS4 (lift / drag) or WS5 (drag coefficient). Mount on the two-force module; sweep angle or fan speed.

If your question is about flow patterns:

Use the method from WS6 (smoke). Mount the object, use the smoke generator and capture stills + slow-motion video.

  2. Hypothesis — predicted answer with brief reasoning
  3. Variables — independent, dependent, controlled
  4. Test article — sketch + dimensions + frontal area check

WS7 · Worked Example06 / 08

Example brief — flap study

QuestionHow does a plain flap deflected 0–30° change the lift coefficient of a NACA 0015 at α = 5°?
HypothesisLift will rise with flap deflection up to ~25°, then separation reduces gains. Drag will rise across the range.
VariablesIV: flap deflection (0°/10°/20°/30°). DV: lift & drag. CV: α = 5°, fan = 50%.
Test article3D-printed NACA 0015 with hinged flap, 25% chord. Frontal area < 10%.
ProcedureAdapted from WS4. Four flap configurations.
Data planThree repeats per flap angle. Plot CL and CD vs flap deflection.
WS7 · Report07 / 08

Report structure

Target ~1,500 words plus figures:

  1. Introduction — context, motivation, the question
  2. Theory — relevant equations and concepts
  3. Method — equipment, procedure, test article, controlled variables
  4. Results — data tables, graphs, annotated images
  5. Discussion — what the data shows; comparison to expectations; uncertainty
