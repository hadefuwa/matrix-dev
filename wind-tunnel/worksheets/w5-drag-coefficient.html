Worksheet 5 Week 6 · Drag Components Learning Aim B

Drag Coefficient

Measure drag on six standard reference shapes — cone, smooth sphere, half-sphere, rough sphere, flat surface and teardrop — and compare experimental Cd values to published data.

Overview01

What you'll do in this worksheet

"Streamlined" is everyday speech for a shape that slips through a fluid with minimum resistance. In engineering we quantify that with the drag coefficient Cd.

You'll measure drag force on six different reference shapes, calculate Cd for each, and compare with the published values used in industry.

Learning objectives

  • Calculate the drag coefficient from measured force, velocity and reference area
  • Compare experimental and theoretical Cd for six standard shapes
  • Explain why a real car's Cd is around 0.2–0.3, and why golf balls are dimpled
  • Understand that Cd can depend on Reynolds number, surface roughness and flow regime
Theory02

The drag-coefficient formula

Cd = D / (½ ρ V² A) D = measured drag force (N) · ρ = 1.20 kg·m⁻³ · V = air speed (m·s⁻¹) · A = frontal area of the body (m²)

For the test shapes here, diameter d = 44 mm = 0.044 m. Frontal area A = πd²/4.

Why drag varies with shape

Two main contributions:

  • Form (pressure) drag — bigger when the body has a large wake (a flat plate or a half-sphere facing the flow). Reduced by streamlining the back of the body.
  • Skin-friction drag — viscous drag along the surface. Smaller for shorter, smoother bodies; bigger for long thin ones.

Procedure04

Per-shape measurement loop

  1. Attach the first shape (start with the cone) to the two-force holder using the threaded shaft.
  2. Place the holder in the test section and secure with thumb-nuts.
  3. Connect both holder sockets to the control unit as in WS4.
  4. Set α = 0° (relative to flow).
  5. With the fan off, zero the sensors on the touch-screen.
  6. Switch the fan on. Set to 10%.
  7. Read and record air speed and drag force.
  8. Step the fan speed up to 100% in 10% increments, recording at every step.
  9. Switch off. Swap to the next shape and repeat.
Time saver Six shapes × ten fan speeds = 60 data points. Allocate roles in the lab pair: one operator, one recorder, one to check zeroing between shapes.
Analysis05

Calculating Cd

  • For each shape and fan speed, calculate Cd using the formula on slide 2.
  • Look at the Cd-vs-velocity profile for each shape. Is Cd independent of velocity? It usually is over a wide range of Reynolds numbers, but watch for surprises with the spheres.
  • Compute a representative (mid-speed) Cd for each shape and enter it in the comparison table on the next slide.

Discussion06

Two classic application questions

Q1 — Modern cars have Cd around 0.2 to 0.3

Looking at your six measured shapes, which would you base a streamlined car body on? Why? Where might you compromise — e.g. the wheels, the windscreen rake, the rear deck?

Q2 — Why are golf balls dimpled?

You measured a rough sphere with markedly lower drag than a smooth sphere. Use this result to explain — in your own words — why dimpling a golf ball lets it travel further.

Hint Think about where on the sphere the boundary layer separates. Roughness triggers a turbulent boundary layer earlier, which stays attached further round the back of the ball — shrinking the wake and the pressure drag.
Extension07

Lift / drag balance and design

Combine your worksheet results so far:

  • From WS4 you have CL-vs-α for three aerofoils. From WS5 you have Cd for six bluff bodies.
  • Pick one realistic vehicle (a glider, a Formula 1 car, a Boeing 787, a wind turbine blade) and discuss which combination of lift-producing and drag-minimising shapes the designers prioritise — and why.

Use your own Cd data, your own L/D ratios from WS4 and any reasonable open-source figures to justify the analysis.
