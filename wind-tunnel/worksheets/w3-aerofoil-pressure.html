Aerofoil Pressure Mapping

Silver Learning Aim B · 9 slides · CP0704
Overview01

What you'll do in this worksheet

When fluid flows around an object, it can generate net forces that cause the object to rise or fall. The shape (profile) the body presents to the flow determines the size of these forces.

An aerofoil is a shape specifically engineered to exploit this effect — giving aircraft lift, wind turbines rotation and ship propellers thrust. In this worksheet you'll measure the pressure at 23 points along a NACA 0015 aerofoil and plot how the pressure varies along its surface.

Learning objectives

  • Measure static pressure at 23 tappings along the chord of an aerofoil
  • Calculate Cp at each tapping and plot Cp vs fractional chord
  • Compare upper- and lower-surface pressure distributions
  • See how angle of attack changes the pressure profile and the lift it produces
Theory02

Why pressure mapping reveals lift

The flow over the upper surface of an aerofoil is accelerated; Bernoulli tells us that means lower pressure there. Beneath the aerofoil, flow is slower and the pressure is higher. The net upward force from this pressure difference is lift.

A pressure-distribution diagram makes this visible: at each tapping you measure how far above or below ambient the local pressure is.

Cp = (P − P∞) / (½ ρ V²) P = pressure at the tapping (Pa) · P∞ = free-stream pressure (Pa, taken from WS1) · ρ = 1.20 kg·m⁻³ · V = free-stream velocity (m·s⁻¹)

NACA 0015 — symmetric profile

The first two digits "00" mean no camber (symmetric); "15" means thickness-to-chord = 15%. At α = 0° it should produce essentially no lift. As α increases, the top-surface pressure drops sharply and lift develops.

Procedure05

Step by step

  1. Place the aerofoil holder into the test section and secure with thumb-nuts.
  2. Set the angle of attack to α = 0°.
  3. Zero the pressure sensors with the fan off.
  4. Switch on the fan and set to 25%.
  5. Connect tapping 1 to port 1 on the controller. Leave port 2 open to ambient.
  6. Read and record the air speed and pressure displayed on the digital meter for that point.
  7. Disconnect tapping 1 and repeat for tappings 2 through 23 in turn.
  8. Now set α = 5° and repeat all 23 readings.
  9. Finally set α = 12° and repeat once more.
Why these angles? 0° is the baseline (no lift), 5° is a typical cruise angle and 12° is close to (or past) the stall point for NACA 0015 — comparing the three lets you see the pressure peak migrate forward and eventually collapse.
Analysis06

From pressure to Cp-vs-x graph

  • For each tapping, use the formula on slide 2 to calculate Cp.
  • Plot Cp vs fractional chord position x. On the same axes, plot top-surface and lower-surface curves.
  • Repeat for α = 5° and α = 12°.
  • Compare all three diagrams.
What to look for
  • The area between the upper and lower Cp curves is proportional to the lift coefficient CL.
  • At α = 0° on a symmetric aerofoil, the two curves should coincide — confirming zero net lift.
  • At α = 12° you should see a sharp suction peak near the leading edge that may have begun to collapse — the onset of stall.
Pressure-distribution diagram07

Sketching the arrows

Use the chord-position template provided in the Student Handout. For each tapping, draw an arrow:

  • Outward arrows (away from the body) for negative Cp — suction
  • Inward arrows (towards the body) for positive Cp

Repeat for each angle. Stick all three diagrams alongside the corresponding Cp-vs-x graphs in your lab notebook.

Challenge

Obtain the same set of distributions at fan speeds of 50%, 80% and 100%. Do the Cp profiles overlay across all four speeds (as Reynolds-number scaling theory predicts)?
