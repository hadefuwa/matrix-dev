Smoke Visualisation

Bronze Learning Aim B · 8 slides · CP0704
Overview01

What you'll do in this worksheet

Wind tunnels are used in industry to visualise the air flow around cars, aircraft and buildings — to improve their design. In this worksheet you'll inject a thin stream of smoke into the tunnel inlet and photograph the patterns produced.

Because the streams change rapidly, you'll capture both still photographs and (better) slow-motion video with a phone camera.

Learning objectives

  • Operate the smoke generator safely and generate clean streamlines
  • Capture flow patterns with stills and slow-motion phone video
  • Identify regions of laminar and turbulent flow
  • Spot flow separation behind bluff bodies and how it shrinks with streamlining
Theory02

What you'll see in the smoke

  • Laminar flow — smoke ribbons stay smooth and parallel. Layers slide cleanly over each other.
  • Transition — the smooth ribbon develops small wobbles. Often happens further back along a body as Reynolds number grows.
  • Turbulent flow — the ribbon breaks up into swirling eddies, mixing rapidly.
  • Separation — flow detaches from the surface behind a bluff body, leaving a low-pressure wake. Visible as a clean streamline that suddenly curves away from the body.
Why this matters Streamlining a body shifts the separation point further back along it — shrinking the wake and reducing form drag. You measured exactly that with Cd in WS5; now you'll see it.
Equipment03

What you'll need

  • Matrix Wind Tunnel 125
  • Smoke generator (oil-vapour mist), correctly filled and warm
  • Two-force module holder (so you can mount any test object firmly)
  • A selection of test objects — pick contrasting pairs (e.g. flat plate vs teardrop, smooth sphere vs rough sphere from the WS5 kit)
  • Phone with a slow-motion video setting

Open the User Manual at p. 17 → for smoke generator warm-up procedure and safety guidance.

Safety The smoke is a fine oil mist. Don't operate the tunnel without the test section secured. Ventilate the room. Avoid prolonged inhalation.
Procedure04

Step by step

The exact procedure depends on what you want to investigate, so the instructions below are general guidelines.

  1. Mount your first test object firmly on the two-force module. Place it in the test section at the desired angle.
  2. Warm up the smoke generator according to the manual.
  3. Switch on the fan at a low fan speed first. (Too fast and the smoke is whipped away before you can see anything useful.)
  4. Hold the smoke generator outlet near the centre of the wind tunnel's inlet grille.
  5. Adjust its position laterally to put the smoke onto the part of the body you want to inspect.
  6. When you have a clear pattern, capture it on your phone. Use slow-motion if available — turbulence at standard 30 fps looks like noise.
  7. Experiment with other fan speeds and other angles of attack.
  8. Switch objects and repeat.
Capturing flow05

Tips for clean imagery

  • Dark backdrop behind the test section — black card outside the rear window makes the smoke pop.
  • Side lighting — a small LED torch shining across the test section perpendicular to your camera highlights the smoke ribbons.
  • Slow-motion video at 120–240 fps stops the eddies clearly.
  • Steady camera — rest the phone or use a tripod. Even small shake makes the streamlines hard to read.
  • One variable at a time — change either the object, the speed or the angle. Keep everything else fixed.
Analysis06

Annotating your captures

For each image / clip, label:

  • Stagnation point — where the front streamline splits onto the upper / lower surface
  • Acceleration region — streamlines tight together (low pressure, high velocity)
  • Boundary layer growth — gradual broadening of the smoke ribbon next to the surface
  • Separation point — where the streamline first peels away from the surface
  • Wake — the disordered region downstream
Linking back to WS5 For each bluff body where you measured Cd, you should now be able to see why a high Cd shape has a wide wake and a low Cd shape has a narrow one.
Investigations07

Suggested studies

  • Streamlining a brick — flow over a flat surface vs the teardrop; locate and compare the separation point.
  • Smooth vs rough sphere — visualise the drag-reduction effect of surface roughness from WS5 directly.
  • Aerofoil at increasing α — start at 0°, work up to 15°+. Watch the flow detach from the upper surface as you approach stall.
  • Tandem bodies — place one body in the wake of another. How does the second body's flow differ from an isolated body?
  • Surface treatment — apply tape strips or sandpaper to a sphere; does that trip the boundary layer to turbulence earlier?
Wrap up08

What you should be able to do now

  • Operate the smoke generator and produce clean, repeatable streamlines
  • Photograph and video flow patterns
  • Identify laminar, transitional and turbulent regions in your captures
  • Connect the visual evidence to the Cd data from WS5 and the pressure data from WS3
