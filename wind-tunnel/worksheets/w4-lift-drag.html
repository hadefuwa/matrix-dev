Lift & Drag on an Aerofoil

Gold Learning Aim B · 9 slides · CP0704
Overview01

What you'll do in this worksheet

WS3 inferred lift from a pressure map. This worksheet measures lift and drag directly with built-in load cells.

  • You'll sweep angle of attack from −25° to +45° in 5° steps
  • At three fan speeds: 20%, 50%, 80%
  • For three different aerofoil profiles: NACA 0015, S1223, NACA 4415
  • Then compare their lift / drag characteristics on the same axes

Learning objectives

  • Use the two-force module to capture lift and drag forces
  • Plot lift- and drag-vs-α curves at multiple air speeds
  • Identify the stall angle from the lift curve
  • Compare three aerofoils and select an appropriate one for a given application
Theory02

The lift equation

For any aerofoil, lift force scales with air density, the square of velocity, the planform area and a dimensionless coefficient:

L = ½ ρ V² S CL S = aerofoil planform area · CL = lift coefficient (depends on shape, angle of attack and Reynolds number)

The drag coefficient CD follows the same pattern. The lift / drag ratio CL/CD is a key efficiency metric — a glider wants a large value, a Formula 1 wing wants the opposite.

Stall

As angle of attack rises, lift grows linearly — until the flow on the top surface separates. CL drops sharply and drag spikes. That angle is the stall angle.

Equipment03

The two-force module

  • Two built-in load cells: one measures lift, the other drag
  • Stepper motor sets the angle of attack precisely
  • Three aerofoils with threaded inserts: NACA 0015 (symmetric), S1223 (high-lift Selig), NACA 4415 (cambered)
  • Cables linking the holder to the wind tunnel control unit

Open the User Manual at pp. 13–14 → for connection diagrams and the aerofoil mounting procedure.

Procedure04

Step by step — NACA 0015

  1. Thread the NACA 0015 aerofoil onto the two-force holder's shaft. Mount it in the test section with thumb-nuts.
  2. Connect both holder sockets to the corresponding sockets on the control unit.
  3. Set α = 0°. With the fan off, zero both load cells.
  4. Switch the fan on and set to 20%.
  5. Sweep α from −25° to +45° in 5° steps. At each angle, record lift force and drag force.
  6. Repeat the full sweep at 50% fan speed.
  7. Repeat once more at 80% fan speed.
  8. Switch off the fan. Swap to the S1223. Repeat steps 3–7.
  9. Swap to the NACA 4415. Repeat once more.
Analysis · NACA 001505

Lift and drag curves

  • For the NACA 0015 only: plot lift force vs α for all three fan speeds on a single set of axes. Label each curve with its speed.
  • On a second set of axes, plot drag force vs α for the same three speeds.
  • Draw smooth curves through the experimental points.

Questions

  • How does lift change with fan speed at fixed α? Does the data agree with the V² scaling in the lift equation?
  • At what angle does the lift on the NACA 0015 peak? What does the drag do at that same angle?
  • Is the lift curve symmetric about α = 0°? Why does that make sense for a symmetric aerofoil?
Comparing aerofoils06

Three aerofoils, side by side

Now collect all three aerofoils' results at the same fan speed (20%):

  • Plot a single graph of lift vs α with three curves (NACA 0015 · S1223 · NACA 4415).
  • Plot a second graph of drag vs α with the same three aerofoils.

Compare

  • Which aerofoil gives the most lift at small positive α?
  • Which stalls earliest? Which has the gentlest stall behaviour?
  • Which has the lowest drag at zero lift? (drag at α where L ≈ 0)
  • Compute the maximum L/D for each — which is most efficient for a glider?
Application07

Picking the right aerofoil for the job

Use your three plots to recommend an aerofoil for each application below, and justify with one or two metrics:

  • A man-powered glider needing maximum L/D at low speed
  • A training light aircraft needing benign, predictable stall
  • A high-speed UAV where minimising drag at small α matters most
  • An aerobatic aircraft needing symmetric behaviour in inverted flight
Engineering judgement Real aircraft choose their aerofoil with many more constraints (structural depth, fuel volume, Mach number). The point of this exercise is to learn how to argue with data — not to find a single "right" answer.
