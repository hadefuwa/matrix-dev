Flow Around a Cylinder

Silver Learning Aim B · 9 slides · CP0704
Overview01

What you'll do in this worksheet

Designers reduce drag on ships, cars and aircraft by shaping the body to control the flow around it. Two factors matter most:

  • The profile the body presents to the flow (geometry)
  • The speed of the flow (captured by Reynolds number)

To quantify how pressure varies around any shape, engineers use the dimensionless pressure coefficient Cp. In this worksheet you measure Cp around a cylinder and produce a pressure-distribution diagram.

Learning objectives

  • Define and calculate the pressure coefficient Cp
  • Record pressure across angle of attack 0° → 180° in 5° steps
  • Produce a Cp-vs-angle graph and a pressure-distribution diagram
  • Compare experimental results to the theoretical inviscid-flow curve
Theory02

Pressure coefficient Cp

Cp compares the local pressure on a surface to the free-stream conditions, non-dimensionalised by the dynamic pressure. It lets results from any tunnel speed or size collapse onto a single curve.

Cp = (P − P∞) / (½ ρ V²) P = pressure at the point on the body (Pa) · P∞ = free-stream pressure (Pa, from WS1) · ρ = 1.20 kg·m⁻³ · V = free-stream velocity (m·s⁻¹)

Reynolds number — why scaling matters

Two bodies of different sizes will produce the same flow pattern only if their Reynolds numbers match. For the cylinder:

Re = ρ V D / μ D = cylinder diameter · μ = dynamic viscosity of air (1.81 × 10⁻⁵ Pa·s)
Equipment03

What you'll need

  • Matrix Wind Tunnel 125
  • Tapped cylinder attached to a circular protractor (with 5° resolution)
  • Two thumb-nuts to secure the holder
  • Digital pressure sensor (built into the controller)
  • WS1 results — the free-stream pressure P∞ for each fan speed
Setup tip Line up the 90° mark on the protractor with the vertical marker on the test window. This sets your zero-angle reference.

Open the User Manual at pp. 7–9 → for the holder fitting diagram and twisting mechanism.

Procedure04

Sweeping the angle of attack

  1. Place the cylinder holder into the test section. Secure with thumb-nuts.
  2. Rotate to angle of attack α = 0° (the 90° protractor mark aligned with the vertical window marker).
  3. Connect the pressure port on the holder to one port on the control unit. Leave the other port open to ambient pressure.
  4. Zero the pressure sensor on the touch-screen before turning on the fan.
  5. Turn the fan on. Set to 20%.
  6. Read and record the air speed and pressure shown on the digital meter.
  7. Rotate the cylinder to α = 5°. Repeat reading.
  8. Continue in 5° steps up to α = 180°, recording at every position.
Analysis05

Calculating Cp and plotting

  • For each angle, use the formula to calculate Cp. Both pressure values should be negative — record magnitudes carefully.
  • Plot a graph of Cp vs angle of attack α for the 20% fan speed.
  • If you use Excel, you may need to convert degrees to radians (1° = 0.01745 rad).

Compare to theory

The theoretical curve for inviscid (potential) flow around a cylinder is:

Cp(α) = 1 − 4 sin²(α)

Compare your measured curve to this. Where do they diverge? Reasons usually include: viscous boundary layers, flow separation behind the cylinder, and wake effects — none of which inviscid theory accounts for.

Pressure Distribution06

Drawing the pressure-distribution diagram

Engineers often present pressure data as arrows around the body:

  • Negative pressure — arrows point away from the body (suction)
  • Positive pressure — arrows point towards the body

Use your Cp values to draw arrows on the template provided in the Student Handout. Your readings cover 0° → 180°; the cylinder is symmetric, so you can mirror the data for the other side.

Why this matters Pressure-distribution diagrams are how aerodynamicists communicate where lift comes from and where drag arises. You'll meet them again in WS3 when you map the pressure around an aerofoil.
Challenge07

Reynolds-number sweep

Repeat the whole procedure at fan speeds of 50%, 80% and 100%. Calculate Reynolds number at each, and overlay the resulting Cp curves on the same axes.

  • Do the curves collapse onto each other? They should — that's the meaning of "non-dimensionalised".
  • If they don't, what does that tell you about the assumptions of inviscid Cp theory?
  • Look for changes in the separation point as Reynolds increases.
Open question for the report At what Reynolds number does the cylinder's wake first show clearly turbulent behaviour in this rig? Cross-check your reasoning against published cylinder-drag-crisis data.
