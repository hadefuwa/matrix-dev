Pitot-static Tube

Bronze Learning Aim A · 9 slides · CP0704
Overview 01

What you'll do in this worksheet

The pitot-static tube measures the speed of a fluid by comparing two pressures: the pitot pressure (the tube pointing into the flow) and the static pressure (around the tube). The faster the air, the larger the difference between them.

Learning objectives

  • Explain how a pitot-static tube measures fluid speed
  • Take air-speed measurements using a U-tube manometer and a digital pressure sensor
  • Apply Bernoulli's equation to convert pressure difference into velocity
  • Calculate the Reynolds number for each operating point
Where this fits Week 3 of the BTEC scheme — the lab that anchors the theory of static, dynamic and total pressure introduced in the Bernoulli lecture.
Theory 02

How a pitot-static tube works

A pitot-static tube has two openings:

  • The pitot port points directly into the air flow. The moving air is brought to rest at this port — its kinetic energy converts into a small extra pressure called dynamic pressure.
  • The static port(s) sit perpendicular to the flow. They sense only the static pressure of the surrounding air.

Subtracting one from the other gives the dynamic pressure on its own, and Bernoulli's equation relates that directly to velocity.

Ptotal = Pstatic + ½ ρ V² Rearranging gives the velocity from the pressure difference Δp = Ptotal − Pstatic:
V = √( 2 Δp / ρ ) where Δp is the pitot-static pressure difference in Pa and ρ is the density of air, taken as 1.20 kg·m⁻³.
Equipment 03

What you'll need

  • Matrix Wind Tunnel 125 with touch-screen controller
  • Pitot-static tube experiment holder + 2 thumb-nuts
  • U-tube manometer (filled with water to about 50 mm)
  • Built-in digital pressure meter on the controller
  • Stopwatch (optional, for steady-state checks)
  • Lab notebook / Student Handout sheet
Safety Never run the fan with the test section unsecured. Keep loose hair, sleeves and cables clear of the inlet grille. Switch off before changing connections.

Open the User Manual at pp. 5–6 → for full set-up photos and port-numbering diagrams.

Procedure · Part 1 04

Reading air speed with a U-tube manometer

  1. Place the pitot-static holder into the test section. Secure with both thumb-nuts. The tube must point directly into the flow.
  2. Add water to the manometer via a side port so the water sits near the 50 mm mark.
  3. Connect the experiment holder to the manometer ports. (Either way round is fine — only the difference in column heights matters.)
  4. With the fan off (0%), record both column heights in the data table.
  5. Switch on the fan, set to 10% on the touch-screen.
  6. Read and record both column heights.
  7. Increase the fan speed in 10% steps up to 100%, recording the column heights at each step.
Analysis · Part 1 05

From column height to air speed

The pressure difference across the manometer is the weight of the water column. For each fan speed:

Δp = ρw · g · h ρw = 1000 kg·m⁻³ · g = 9.81 N·kg⁻¹ · h = height difference in metres

Substituting into the velocity expression gives:

V² = 2 ρw · g · h / ρair with ρair = 1.20 kg·m⁻³

For each fan-speed value, calculate h in metres and then V in m·s⁻¹. Plot a graph of air speed against height difference and draw a smooth curve through the points.

Procedure · Part 2 06

Reading air speed with the digital sensor

  1. Detach the tubes from the manometer. Connect them to the two pressure ports on the wind tunnel control unit. (Again, polarity does not matter — you'll work with the magnitude.)
  2. With the fan off, zero the sensors from the touch-screen.
  3. Set the fan to 10%. Read air speed and pressure difference from the digital meter.
  4. Step the fan up to 100% in 10% increments, recording both readings each time.
Why both methods? Comparing the manometer (manual, mechanical) with the digital sensor (electronic) trains learners in the trade-off between resolution, response time and human-reading error — a key BTEC technical skill.
Analysis · Part 2 07

Plotting and comparing

  • Plot a second graph: air speed vs pressure difference, this time from the digital pressure meter.
  • Draw a smooth curve through the points.
  • Compare the two graphs. They should agree within experimental error — comment on which method is more precise and which is faster to use.

Challenge — Reynolds number

For each fan-speed percentage, calculate the Reynolds number:

Re = ρ V L / μ ρ = 1.20 kg·m⁻³ · μ = 1.81 × 10⁻⁵ Pa·s (dynamic viscosity of air) · L = the chord or characteristic length of the test object

Comment on the regime (laminar, transitional, turbulent) the tunnel achieves at different fan settings.

Wrap up 09

What you should be able to do now

  • Explain in your own words why a pitot-static tube measures velocity, not pressure
  • Take readings using both manometer and digital sensor — and justify which you'd choose for which application
  • Apply Bernoulli's equation in both algebraic and numerical form
  • Calculate Reynolds number and explain its significance for flow regime
